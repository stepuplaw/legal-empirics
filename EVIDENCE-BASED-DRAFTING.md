# Evidence-based drafting

A position paper.

*Draft. The empirical claims below are testable and several are not yet tested.
Where that is so, it is said.*

## 1. The claim

Most litigation over private instruments is not caused by bad faith or by
genuinely novel facts. It is caused by a small, recurring set of drafting
failures and predictable factual situations that the drafter did not anticipate.
Those failures leave a record. Every time a court construes a will, a trust, or
an agreement, it quotes the language that failed and explains why.

That record can be assembled and measured. Once it can be measured, the
recurring failures can be named. Once they are named, a drafter can avoid them.

The claim, then, is narrow and empirical: **the failure modes of private legal
instruments are finite, recurrent, and identifiable from the public record.**

Everything else here follows from that.

## 2. Why this is possible now and was not before

Nothing about the idea is new. Every experienced practitioner carries an
informal version of it, learned from their own cases and from war stories. What
is new is that the informal version can be replaced by a measured one.

Three things changed. Bulk judicial opinion data became publicly available at
scale. Full-text indexing made ten million opinions searchable in under a
second on ordinary hardware. And the cost of reading and classifying a few
thousand passages fell far enough that one practitioner can do work that used
to require a funded research team.

The constraint was never the idea. It was that nobody could afford to look.

## 3. The precedent: evidence-based medicine

Medicine made this transition and the argument was the same.

Before roughly the middle of the twentieth century, clinical practice rested on
individual judgment, training lineage, and accumulated personal experience. A
good doctor was one with good instincts and long practice. The evidence-based
medicine movement did not claim those doctors were foolish. It claimed something
narrower and harder to answer: that when systematically aggregated evidence
exists and contradicts individual impression, the evidence should win, because
individual impression is built on a small and unrepresentative sample.

The moral force came later and followed automatically. Once the evidence was
obtainable, practising without consulting it stopped being neutral. It became a
choice with consequences for a patient.

Legal drafting sits today roughly where medicine sat before that shift. A good
drafter is one with good instincts and long practice, working from a sample of
cases that is small, local, and shaped by which matters happened to reach their
desk. That is not a criticism of the drafter. It is a description of the
information available to them.

**Evidence-based drafting** is the same proposal, in the same shape: where
measured evidence about how instruments fail exists, it should inform how
instruments are written.

## 4. The moral argument

This is the part that matters, and it should be stated carefully rather than
loudly.

A family that litigates a will has already lost. They lose money that was meant
for them, they lose years, and frequently they lose each other. Siblings who
fight over an estate often do not reconcile. The cost is not only the fee award.
It is a permanent change in a family.

Much of that is avoidable. When a court construes an ambiguous provision, it is
resolving a dispute the drafter could often have foreclosed with different
words. The dispute exists because of a document.

So the argument runs:

1. A meaningful share of instrument litigation is caused by identifiable
   drafting failures.
2. Those failures are visible in the public record and can be catalogued.
3. Cataloguing them is now cheap.
4. Therefore, where the catalogue exists, drafting without regard to it imposes
   an avoidable and foreseeable harm on people who trusted the drafter.

Point four is the moral claim, and it only holds once points one through three
are actually done. That is the obligation this work accepts. It is not enough to
assert that the knowledge is obtainable. Somebody has to obtain it and publish
it, freely, so that the standard is available to every practitioner and not only
to those who can pay for it.

That is why the outputs here are openly licensed. A finding kept private would
make the argument self-serving and would not reduce anything.

## 5. What this is not

It is not a claim that all litigation is preventable. Some disputes are genuine
contests over real interests, and some parties would fight over a perfectly
drafted document.

It is not a claim that a machine should draft. The finding is an input to
professional judgment, not a replacement for it.

It is not a claim to certainty. Language is irreducibly imperfect and no
instrument can foreclose every reading.

And it is not, yet, a causal claim. See section 6.

## 6. What the data can and cannot support

Being honest about this is the difference between a research programme and a
marketing document.

**Published appellate opinions are a severely biased sample.** Most disputes
never become suits. Most suits settle. Most settlements leave no public record.
Most trials are not appealed. Not every appeal is published. The cases that
survive all of those filters cluster around genuine uncertainty, which is
precisely what makes them unrepresentative of disputes generally. This is the
classic selection problem in litigation data, associated with Priest and Klein.

**The corpus holds only the litigated half.** To say that a phrase *causes*
litigation, one needs a comparison group of instruments containing that phrase
that were never litigated. That group is not in the corpus. Without it, a
frequency table of disputed language shows what recurs in litigated instruments,
which may partly reflect what recurs in instruments generally. If a common
formula dominates the disputed language, that may only mean it is common.

**So the defensible claim is narrower than the ambition.** What can be published
now is a catalogue of judicially construed language: these provisions required a
court to determine their meaning, here is how often, and here is what the court
said went wrong. That is drafting intelligence. It is genuinely useful and it is
new. It is not yet a risk model, and calling it one would be false.

Closing that gap requires better data, which is the next section.

## 7. The data policy problem

The appellate record is the smallest and least representative part of the
judicial record, and it is the only part that is genuinely accessible.

What is missing is the trial level: complaints, answers, motions for summary
judgment, the exhibits attaching the actual instruments, and the orders
resolving them. That is where most disputes are actually decided, where the
disputed documents themselves appear, and where the reasoning is most concrete.
It is largely locked behind per-page federal court access fees, and state trial
court records are scattered across county clerks in formats that vary by
jurisdiction.

The consequence is structural. Empirical work on how legal instruments fail is
confined to the thin, biased slice that is free to read. The research is shaped
by the paywall rather than by the question.

**The policy position:** federal court records are public records, produced by a
public institution at public expense, and access to them should not be metered.
Bulk, machine-readable access to district and bankruptcy court filings and
orders would allow the questions in this document to be answered properly rather
than approximately. The cost of providing it is a budgeting decision, and it is
small relative to the social cost of the litigation the resulting knowledge
could prevent.

That is not a novel position. In *National Veterans Legal Services Program v.
United States*, 968 F.3d 1340, 1357 (Fed. Cir. 2020), the court read the statute
to limit PACER fees to what is needed to cover the cost of providing public
access; the case settled for $125,000,000 and the Federal Circuit affirmed final
approval on 2026-03-20.

**Legislatively, however, the door is currently shut.** The Open Courts Act died
three times, twice in the 117th Congress and once after passing the House in the
116th, and **no successor has been introduced in the 118th or 119th Congress.**
Fees remain ten cents a page. Anyone writing that reform legislation is pending
is wrong, and the absence of a live bill is itself part of the argument. The contribution this
work can make is to demonstrate concretely what becomes answerable when data is
open, and what stays unanswerable while it is not. A limitations section that
says "this could not be determined because the records are behind a paywall" is
itself an argument.

## 8. Why agreements are worth this much trouble

The instinct that agreements deserve care is very old, and it is worth saying
where it comes from rather than treating it as self-evident.

Genesis records Abraham twice doing something that reads oddly to a modern eye,
and both times the oddity is the point.

At Beersheba, Abraham and Avimelech have a live dispute over a well that
Avimelech's servants had seized. They resolve it, and then they do something
more. They make a covenant fixing the position going forward, and Abraham sets
seven ewe lambs apart as a witness that the well was his. The name of the place
records the oath. The dispute is settled, and the mechanism for settling it is
memorialised so it cannot recur on the same facts. The parties did not stop at
resolving the conflict. They engineered against its repetition.

At Machpelah, Sarah has died and Abraham needs a burial place. Ephron offers the
field as a gift, in front of witnesses. Abraham refuses the gift and insists on
paying the full price in current commercial money, publicly, before everyone who
came to the gate of the city. The text then says the field and the cave were
made sure to Abraham as a possession. He is buying certainty rather than land.
He takes what looks like the worse deal, paying for what was offered free,
because a gift can be disputed by a later generation and a witnessed purchase at
full price cannot. It is the first estate planning transaction in the Western
canon, and it is deliberately over-documented.

Both stories say the same thing. The work of making an agreement clear is not
overhead. It is the substance. A society in which people can rely on what they
have agreed is qualitatively different from one in which they cannot, and the
difference is built one carefully drafted instrument at a time.

That is the tradition this argument sits in. The proposal is only that we now
have tools to find out, empirically, which words have failed to provide the
certainty Abraham was buying, and an obligation to use them.

## 9. Where this sits in the literature

This is not a new discipline. It is the joining of two existing ones that have
not previously been put together, which is a better position to occupy than
inventing a field, because both halves already have standards, journals and
critics.

**The home discipline is empirical legal studies.** Its deep root is
*jurimetrics*, named by Lee Loevinger in 1949 and still the title of an ABA
journal. The modern institutions are the Journal of Empirical Legal Studies and
the annual Conference on Empirical Legal Studies. That is where the methods in
[METHODOLOGY.md](METHODOLOGY.md) come from and where work like this is
criticised.

**The normative ancestor is preventive law.** Louis M. Brown argued from about
1950 that the lawyer's proper work is preventing disputes rather than winning
them, and that the profession had inverted its priorities. Preventive law was
absorbed later into therapeutic jurisprudence. It is a serious tradition with
one persistent weakness: it stayed normative. It told practitioners to prevent
problems without ever measuring which problems recur, so its advice remained at
the level of experienced judgment.

**The contribution is the join.** Preventive law supplies the aim. Empirical
legal studies supplies the apparatus. Preventive law never had an empirical
engine, largely because until recently there was no affordable way to build one.
Call it **empirical preventive law**, or evidence-based drafting, but the
framing that matters is that it is an old and respectable goal finally given
measurement.

Method is borrowed rather than invented, deliberately: systematic content
analysis from Hall and Wright, corpus techniques from the legal corpus
linguistics literature, and the selection-effect discipline from Priest and
Klein. Borrowing means a critic must attack the application rather than the
method, which is a much better argument to have.

**Two process standards worth adopting rather than improvising.** For the
analytical pipeline, **CRISP-DM** is the long-standing cross-industry process
model in data science: business understanding, data understanding, data
preparation, modelling, evaluation, deployment, iterating rather than running
once. For the published outputs, the **FAIR principles** (findable, accessible,
interoperable, reusable) are the recognised standard for research data. The DOI,
the ORCID, the open licence, the machine-readable formats and the persistent
canonical URL already satisfy most of FAIR, so it is worth claiming compliance
explicitly rather than accidentally.

**Adjacent work worth knowing, and the honest scope of the claim.** Corpus
methods have already been applied to private instruments: Mouritsen's *Contract
Interpretation with Corpus Linguistics*, 94 Wash. L. Rev. 1337 (2019), and
courts applying it to contracts, insurance policies and benefit plans. That
ground is taken.

What a database search across three services did **not** find is any
corpus-linguistic work on **wills, trusts and deeds**. That is the open band,
and the claim in this paper is confined to it. Computational legal studies and
legal analytics work the same corpora with more engineering; empirical contract
design studies commercial agreement structure; legal design is
practitioner-facing but works from user research rather than case data. None
addresses testamentary instruments, which is the reason for doing this.

## 10. The programme

In order, weakest claim to strongest. Each stage must be finished honestly
before the next is credible.

1. **Prevalence.** How often courts engage particular doctrines, measured as
   rates against a proper denominator. Begun.
2. **Construed-language catalogue.** The actual words courts have had to
   interpret, extracted from the opinions that quote them, with frequency and
   collocation.
3. **Outcome coding.** Who prevails, on what evidence, coded against a written
   codebook with reported reliability. This is where the first practically
   useful numbers appear, and it requires reading rather than searching.
4. **Factor extraction.** The recurring factual situations that produce
   disputes, as distinct from the words. Avoidable scenarios rather than
   avoidable phrases.
5. **Structural analysis.** Which agreement architectures fail, as opposed to
   which clauses.
6. **Risk identification.** Only reachable with trial court data and a control
   group of unlitigated instruments. Section 7 is a precondition, not an aside.

## 11. What would falsify this

The claim in section 1 fails if the language courts construe turns out to be
essentially random, showing no recurring patterns beyond the base rate of those
constructions in instruments generally. If the catalogue in stage 2 produces a
flat distribution with no concentration, there is nothing to teach and this
document is wrong.

That is a real possibility and the study is designed so the answer can come back
negative. A programme that cannot fail is not a research programme.
